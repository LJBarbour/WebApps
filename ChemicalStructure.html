Guide

Three ways to get a structure onto the canvas

  • Draw it with the tools in the left rail: bonds, chains, rings, atoms, functional groups and templates.
  • Search for it: press Ctrl K and type a name (for example caffeine, ibuprofen, alanine, toluene) or paste a SMILES string.
  • Import it: open or drop a Molfile, SDF, SMILES or saved drawing, or paste SMILES/Molfile text straight onto the canvas with Ctrl V.

What happens next

Every change is analysed straight away. The specimen label at the top left of the canvas shows the compound's name, formula and molar mass. The analysis deck on the right has predicted 1H and 13C NMR, IR and mass spectra, and the properties, stereochemistry and machine-readable codes.

If the canvas holds more than one molecule (for example a reaction scheme), the deck analyses one of them. Click any atom or bond of a molecule to make it the one being analysed; a faint frame marks it.

Hover over an atom to see its hybridisation and predicted shifts. Hover over a row in any deck table, or a peak in a spectrum, to highlight the atoms responsible. It works the other way too: hovering over an atom highlights its signals.

Exporting pictures

Copy puts a high-resolution PNG with a transparent background and black lines on the clipboard, ready to paste into Word, PowerPoint or a manuscript. Export saves the same image as PNG or SVG, or saves the structure as a Molfile or SMILES string. The drawing is autosaved in this browser.

Hover shortcuts

With the pointer over an atom, type an element key to change it: C N O S P F I H, L for Cl, B for Br. + and - change the charge and Enter edits the label. With the pointer over a bond, 1 2 3 set the bond order and W / H make it a wedge or hash bond. Delete removes whatever is under the pointer.

Working with a selection

When atoms are selected, a small toolbar appears under them with: rotate by 15°, flip horizontally or vertically, clean up, expand an abbreviation into its full structure, duplicate and delete. You can also drag the round handle above the selection to rotate freely: it snaps to 15° (hold Shift for any angle) and shows the angle turned so far. Or press [ and ]. For an exact angle, use the rotate-by button or Shift R and type the angle in degrees; positive turns anticlockwise, so 120 and −120 give the two other positions of a threefold arrangement.

If the toolbar is in the way, drag it by the dotted grip at its left end. It then stays where you put it, even for later selections, and the grip turns accent-coloured. Double-click the grip to make the toolbar follow the selection again.

Building from repeated fragments

To make a molecule with several identical parts, such as a threefold (triangular) one, draw one part, including the atom it shares with the others. Duplicate it (Ctrl D), rotate the copy (Shift R, 120), and drag the copy until its shared atom lies on the original's. When a dragged fragment comes close to atoms it would cover, it snaps onto them, and each atom that will be merged is marked with a dashed ring. Releasing the mouse merges the marked pairs into single atoms, joining the fragments, and a toast reports how many were merged. Where two bonds end up joining the same pair of atoms, one is kept, with the higher bond order. Atoms bonded to each other are never merged. Hold Shift while dragging to move a fragment freely without snapping or merging. For fragments that already overlap, the Merge button appears in the selection toolbar; Merge overlapping atoms in the command palette (Shift M) does the same for the selection, or for the whole drawing when nothing is selected. Ctrl Z undoes a merge.

Flipping and mirror images

Flip horizontally or vertically (toolbar buttons, Shift H / Shift V, or the command palette) mirrors the selection, or the whole drawing when nothing is selected, including arrows and text positions. On paper a mirror image with unchanged wedges would be the enantiomer. The flip therefore also swaps every wedge and hash bond at the flipped stereocentres, which amounts to turning the molecule over (a 180° rotation about the mirror line), so R/S descriptors and E/Z geometry stay exactly as they were. To make the enantiomer deliberately, use Enantiomer in the selection toolbar or the command palette. It inverts every wedged stereocentre in place without moving any atoms.

Clean up

Clean up redraws the selected molecules, or everything when nothing is selected, with ideal bond lengths and angles. It keeps the molecule roughly where it was and in the same orientation.

Macrocycles that carry fused rings, such as calixarenes, cyclophanes and benzo-crown ethers, are not drawn as a regular polygon, which would distort the small rings. Each benzene (or other small ring) keeps its own shape and sits outside the macrocycle. The linking atoms (the CH2 groups of a calixarene) take up the rest of the angle, and the ring is turned so that one of the small rings sits on an axis. Calix[4]arene therefore comes out in its usual drawing: four phenols at top, right, bottom and left, with the OH groups pointing inwards. A plain macrocycle, such as a crown ether without benzo rings, is still drawn as a regular polygon.

Copy, paste and duplicate

Ctrl C copies the selection inside the app and also puts its SMILES on the system clipboard. Ctrl V pastes it back, or, if the clipboard holds SMILES or Molfile text from elsewhere, imports that structure. Ctrl D duplicates the selection.

Editing labels

Double-click an atom (or hover over it and press Enter) to type a new label. Labels can be elements with or without hydrogens (N, NH, CH3), charged atoms (N+, O-), or abbreviations. Labels are drawn the right way round for the side the bond comes from: CO2H on the right becomes HO2C on the left.

Valence problems

An atom with too many bonds for its element and charge gets a red dashed ring, and the problem is listed in the Overview tab. Click the warning to select the atom.

Delocalised bonds

Groups whose charge is shared over several atoms are drawn with delocalised bonds, a solid line with a dashed line inside the group, and their net charge shown once beside the group instead of on one atom. This covers carboxylates (R–CO2−), nitro groups and nitrate, and amidinium and guanidinium ions. A nitro group is neutral overall, so it shows no charge. Only the drawing changes: SMILES, names, formulae and spectra still use the ordinary structure with one C=O and one C–O−. For a structure imported from a crystal file, a group is drawn delocalised only if its bond lengths really are equal (within 0.05 Å); a carboxylic acid, or an asymmetric carboxylate, keeps its localised bonds. The Deloc button in the bottom bar turns this off. The ± button next to it hides the group charges but keeps the delocalised bonds, for example to draw a coordinated carboxylate or a partial structure without a formal charge. The drawing still has its charges underneath, so formula, mass and SMILES are unchanged. The delocalised bond tool (in the bond menu) switches single bonds between the two drawings; drawing a new bond with it gives a delocalised single bond.

Metal complexes

A bond from a ligand atom to a metal counts towards the ligand's valence only when the ligand needs it. A pyridine or imidazole N, an amine, an ether or water O already has its full valence, so its bond to the metal is taken as dative (a donated lone pair): the N stays neutral and is not flagged. A Cl, or a carboxylate or alkoxide O, needs the bond, so it counts as a normal covalent bond, and the ligand is drawn neutral without extra hydrogens. Metals have no fixed valence in a complex and are never flagged. Crystal structures (CIF, SHELX) are read the same way: bond orders and charges are worked out for the organic ligands without their metal bonds, so a Cu-bound imidazole keeps its ring double bond instead of becoming an imidazolium.

Overview

The name (see Naming), molecular formula, average molar mass, monoisotopic mass, elemental analysis, degree of unsaturation (DBE), topological polar surface area (TPSA, Ertl method), hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, rotatable bonds, fraction of sp3 carbons and ring counts. It also gives three of Lipinski's rule-of-five checks (logP is not estimated), every stereocentre and stereogenic double bond with its CIP descriptor, and any drawing problems.

1H NMR

A simulated first-order spectrum at the spectrometer frequency you choose, with the residual solvent and water peaks of the chosen solvent. Integrals can be shown. Drag across the spectrum to zoom and double-click to reset. In D2O and CD3OD, exchangeable OH/NH/COOH protons are removed. The table lists the shift, multiplicity, coupling constants, integration and assignment.

13C NMR

A proton-decoupled stick spectrum, or DEPT-135 (CH and CH3 up, CH2 down, quaternary carbons absent) or DEPT-90 (CH only). The solvent signal is shown in grey. Quaternary carbons are drawn weaker, as they usually are in routine spectra.

IR

A simulated transmittance spectrum from 4000 to 400 cm−1 built from characteristic group frequencies, with the strongest bands labelled. The table gives each band's position, strength and assignment.

MS

The molecular-ion isotope pattern (calculated from natural isotope abundances), common ESI adduct m/z values, and typical EI fragment ions for the functional groups present.

3D

A three-dimensional model of the analysed molecule (or of everything on the canvas), with all hydrogens. Drag with the mouse to rotate it in any direction, Shift-drag to roll it in the plane of the screen, use the wheel to zoom and double-click to reset the view. Once the viewer has been clicked, the arrow keys also rotate it. Spin turns the model slowly and continuously. Choose Ball & stick (atoms at 0.2× their van der Waals radius, 0.15× for hydrogen), Sticks or Space-filling (full van der Waals spheres). H hides or shows the hydrogens and Labels adds element and atom numbers. Double and triple bonds are drawn as two or three cylinders. A double bond's pair lies in the plane of its substituents, so it opens up when seen face-on and closes when seen edge-on, as the real π system would. The view is orthographic, so atoms keep their size whatever their depth. Depth cues adds mild perspective and depth fog for those who prefer them. H-bonds shows hydrogen bonds as dashed lines, from H to the acceptor, or from the donor when hydrogens are hidden. A hydrogen bond is counted when the donor and acceptor are N, O or F, H···A is shorter than the sum of their van der Waals radii minus 0.12 Å, the D–H···A angle is at least 120°, and the acceptor is at least four bonds from the H or in another molecule. The Geometry card lists each one with its H···A and D···A distances and angle. For an imported crystal structure viewed as the whole drawing with file coordinates, this includes the hydrogen bonds between the molecules in the file. In idealised models the hydrogen positions are idealised too, so their H-bond geometry is only indicative. Save image writes the current view as a PNG at three times screen resolution with a transparent background. The model can be saved as PDB, SDF or XYZ. New conformer starts the model from a different random geometry, which is useful for flexible molecules and rings. For structures read from a PDB or XYZ file, File coordinates shows and exports the file's own atom positions, for example a crystal structure, and Idealised rebuilds the model from the drawing.

Codes

Canonical SMILES and an MDL Molfile (V2000) for the analysed molecule, both ready to copy. There is also an import box for SMILES, Molfile, PDB or XYZ text, and a name lookup for the built-in compound library.

Please read before relying on the numbers

All spectra are predictions from empirical additivity rules. They are not quantum-chemical calculations and not database look-ups. Use them for teaching, for sanity-checking an assignment, or for a first guess, never in place of measured data.

1H shifts

Protons on sp3 carbons use base values with α- and β-substituent increments (Shoolery/Pretsch-type tables) plus ring corrections. Alkene protons use the Pascual–Meier–Simon increments, with cis/trans taken from the 2D drawing. Aromatic protons start from the parent ring (benzene, pyridine, furan, thiophene, pyrrole and others) and add ortho/meta/para substituent increments. OH, NH and COOH protons get typical broad values; these depend strongly on concentration, solvent and temperature. For simple mono- and difunctional molecules expect about ±0.3 ppm, and more for crowded, strained or strongly conjugated systems.

Multiplicity

Couplings come from first-order n+1 rules using typical 3J values (vicinal ~7 Hz, cis/trans alkene 10/17 Hz, aromatic ortho ~8 Hz). Chemically equivalent protons are found from the molecular graph (topological symmetry). Second-order effects, magnetic non-equivalence, long-range couplings and diastereotopic protons are not modelled.

13C shifts

Grant–Paul-type increments for alkanes, group increments for substituted carbons, dedicated rules for carbonyl, nitrile, alkene and alkyne carbons, and parent-ring values with substituent increments for aromatic carbons. Typical accuracy is ±5 ppm (±6 for carbonyls).

IR bands

Characteristic group frequencies with corrections for conjugation, ring strain (for example cyclopentanone and lactones), hydrogen bonding and substitution pattern (aromatic out-of-plane bends). Positions are usually within ±15–30 cm−1. Intensities are qualitative only.

3D models

The 3D model is built from the drawing, not from a quantum-chemical or force-field optimisation. Every bond gets a standard length for its elements, order and hybridisation (for example C–C 1.53, aromatic C–C 1.39, C=O 1.21, C–H 1.09 Å). Every angle gets its ideal value (109.5° sp³, 120° sp², 180° sp, with ring-size corrections such as 60° in cyclopropane and 108° in five-membered aromatic rings). Aromatic rings, double bonds, amides and other sp² centres are kept planar. The cis/trans arrangement of double bonds and the configuration of wedged stereocentres are taken from the drawing, and atoms more than two bonds apart are kept from touching. The coordinates are then relaxed until these targets are met, usually within 0.01 Å and 1–2°. The result is a sensible, idealised geometry. Saturated rings settle into chair- or envelope-like shapes and chains are staggered, but it is not the lowest-energy conformer. Use it as a starting model or an illustration, and run a proper force field or DFT optimisation where accurate geometry matters. The Geometry card reports how well the targets were met.

Reading SHELX .res and .ins files

The unit cell (CELL), lattice type (LATT), symmetry operators (SYMM), scattering factors (SFAC) and the atom list are read. The atom list is read up to HKLF or END, skipping Q-peaks and the minor components of disorder (PART 2 and above). Coordinates fixed with SHELX's "+10" convention are handled. Fractional coordinates are converted to Cartesian ones with the standard orthogonalisation (a along x, b in the xy plane).

Molecules are then completed by symmetry. Every image of an asymmetric-unit atom, under all the symmetry operators, the lattice centring and the inversion implied by a centrosymmetric LATT, is added if it is covalently bonded to an atom already present, and this repeats until nothing new is found. Molecules on inversion centres, mirror planes or rotation axes, and molecules split across the asymmetric unit, therefore come out whole. Hydrogen bonds and other contacts are never used for this. If the images keep bonding to each other, as in a coordination polymer, the app stops and uses the asymmetric unit alone, and says so. Command palette entries re-import the last SHELX or CIF file either as the asymmetric unit only or completed. Bond orders and charges are then deduced as for PDB files, from the hydrogen count when the file has hydrogens (as refined structures usually do).

Reading CIF files

CIFs are treated exactly like SHELX files, using the same symmetry completion. The first data block that contains atom sites is used, and the message says so if the file holds several structures. The cell comes from _cell_length_a … _cell_angle_gamma (standard uncertainties such as 12.345(2) are ignored). The symmetry operators come from _space_group_symop_operation_xyz or the older _symmetry_equiv_pos_as_xyz; these list every operator, including centring and inversion. The atoms come from the _atom_site loop: label, type symbol and fractional coordinates. Atoms in disorder groups 2 and above and dummy atoms (_atom_site_calc_flag dum) are left out. Quoted values, semicolon text fields and comments are handled. If a CIF has no symmetry operators, only the asymmetric unit can be shown. The re-import commands in the command palette work for CIFs as well.

Missing O–H hydrogens

Crystal structures often include every C–H hydrogen but leave out the hydrogens on O (and sometimes N), which are harder to locate. An O with one bond and no hydrogen would then read as an anion (a phenolate or alkoxide). If nothing positive in the file balances that charge, the O is taken as OH instead, and the import message says so. A carboxylate stays an anion when its two C–O bonds are nearly the same length, as in a real carboxylate; it becomes COOH only when one C–O is clearly longer. In the 3D tab the missing hydrogens are added at the tetrahedral angle, each turned towards the nearest free O or N acceptor. The Geometry card counts them as "placed", and the hydrogen-bond table uses them. A ring of OH groups, as in a calixarene, then forms its cyclic chain of O–H···O bonds.

3D Molfiles with unassigned bond orders

Programs that export crystal structures, such as Mercury, often write every bond of a 3D Molfile as single. When a 3D Molfile has only single bonds but some atoms are then short of their valence, the app ignores the file's bond orders. It works them out as for a PDB file: from the hydrogen count, or without hydrogens from bond lengths, linearity and ring planarity. Flat six-membered rings become aromatic and a short, linear C–N becomes a nitrile. A Molfile whose bond orders are consistent is read as it stands.

Reading PDB and XYZ files

Atoms are read from ATOM/HETATM records. The element comes from columns 77–78, or failing that from the atom name. Bonds come from CONECT records, or failing that from interatomic distances, using the CSD rule of covalent radii plus 0.4 Å. If the file contains hydrogens, bond orders and formal charges follow from each atom's valence, so NH₃⁺, carboxylates and isolated halide ions are recognised. Without hydrogens they are deduced from bond lengths, angles and ring planarity, which is reliable for most organic molecules but can be ambiguous (COOH versus COO⁻, or tautomers). Double-bond geometry and stereocentres in the file are carried into the drawing as E/Z geometry and wedge bonds. Only the first model and the first alternate location of disordered atoms are used.

Masses

Formula, average and monoisotopic masses and isotope patterns use IUPAC atomic weights and isotope abundances, so these are exact calculations rather than predictions. EI fragments are the typical cleavages for the groups present (α-cleavage, benzylic/tropylium, McLafferty, loss of halogen, water or methyl); real spectra may not show all of them.

How names are found

First the structure is compared with a built-in library of about 230 common compounds (solvents, reagents, parent heterocycles, amino acids, nucleobases, familiar drugs and natural products). A match gives the IUPAC name and the common name. Otherwise the systematic namer builds an IUPAC-style (2013 recommendations) substitutive name. It picks the principal characteristic group (acid > ester > acid halide > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > thiol > amine) and the parent chain or ring, numbers it to give the lowest locants, and lists the prefixes alphabetically with multiplying prefixes.

What the systematic namer covers

  • Acyclic compounds, including branched and unsaturated chains
  • Monocyclic carbocycles (cycloalkanes, cycloalkenes)
  • Substituted benzenes, with retained names such as phenol, aniline, benzoic acid, benzaldehyde and benzonitrile, and substituted biphenyls (4,4′-dichloro-1,1′-biphenyl)
  • About 45 heterocyclic and fused parents with their fixed IUPAC numbering, as parents or substituents (for example quinolin-8-ol, 5-bromo-1H-indole, 1-methylpiperidine, 2-(morpholin-4-yl)ethan-1-ol, 1-(pyridin-4-yl)ethan-1-one). Aromatic: pyridine, the diazines, furan, thiophene, pyrrole, the azoles, triazoles and tetrazole. Saturated: piperidine, pyrrolidine, morpholine, piperazine, oxolane, oxane, dioxane and others. Fused: naphthalene, quinoline, isoquinoline, quinoxaline, quinazoline, indole, benzofuran, benzothiophene, benzimidazole, benzoxazole, benzothiazole, indazole and carbazole.
  • Esters, N-substituted amides and amines, ethers, sulfones, nitro and halogen compounds, and simple salts

Limits

Other fused systems (anthracene, phenanthrene, purine…), bridged and spiro systems, and lactones and lactams are named only when the compound itself is in the library. Otherwise the app says the name could not be determined and why. For N–H heterocycles the drawn position of the hydrogen fixes the numbering (indicated hydrogen), as IUPAC requires. Stereodescriptors are added as a prefix when the molecule has exactly one defined stereocentre or stereogenic double bond; with more than one, the descriptors are listed separately in the Overview. The names are carefully produced, but please check any name that is going into print.

Opening files

Open (or drag and drop onto the canvas) a drawing saved from this app (.json), an MDL Molfile (.mol, V2000), the first structure of an SD file (.sdf), a SMILES file (.smi, .txt), or 3D coordinates as a CIF (.cif), a SHELX file (.res, .ins), PDB (.pdb, .ent) or XYZ (.xyz). 3D files are turned into a clean 2D schematic with wedges and E/Z geometry taken from the coordinates. You can also paste any of these as text onto the canvas.

Which 3D format?

  • SDF / MDL Molfile is the best choice for a small molecule. It stores the element, the 3D coordinates, every bond with its order, and formal charges, and nearly every chemistry program reads it, including Mercury, Avogadro, PyMOL, Chimera, Open Babel and RDKit.
  • PDB is the most widely accepted format, especially by structural-biology and visualisation software. It has no bond orders, so the app writes CONECT records for connectivity, plus element symbols (columns 77–78) and formal charges (columns 79–80). Programs then have to guess the bond orders.
  • XYZ is the simplest: just elements and coordinates, with no bonds at all. Convenient as input for quantum-chemistry programs.

Exporting

  • PNG: 3× resolution, transparent background, black lines and labels.
  • SVG: the same drawing as vector graphics.
  • Molfile / SMILES: the molecule being analysed, with abbreviations expanded.
  • 3D model (PDB, SDF, XYZ): the model shown in the 3D tab, with explicit hydrogens.
  • Spectra report: a standalone HTML page with the structure, name, properties and all predicted spectra, which you can print or save as PDF.

Everything stays on this computer

The app is one self-contained file and works offline. Nothing you draw is sent anywhere. The current drawing and your preferences are kept in this browser's local storage.
