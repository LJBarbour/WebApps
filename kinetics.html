Thermodynamics tells you whether a reaction will go; kinetics tells you how fast. The central object of kinetics is the rate law, an empirical (sometimes deducible) relationship between the instantaneous rate of a reaction and the concentrations of species involved.

For a generic reaction \( aA + bB \rightarrow \text{products} \), the rate is often written

General rate law $$ r = -\tfrac{1}{a}\frac{d[A]}{dt} = -\tfrac{1}{b}\frac{d[B]}{dt} = k\,[A]^{m}[B]^{n} $$

where \(k\) is the rate constant, and the exponents \(m, n\) are the partial orders with respect to each reactant. For an elementary step — one that proceeds in a single molecular event — the exponents equal the stoichiometric coefficients (the molecularity). For overall reactions assembled from many elementary steps, the orders must be determined experimentally.

What follows treats the canonical cases. Every panel is live; experiment with it.

Chapter 01

First-order kinetics.

An elementary step \( A \rightarrow P \) in which the rate depends linearly on a single reactant concentration is first-order:

Differential form $$ -\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k\,[A] $$

Separation of variables yields the integrated rate law, an exponential decay:

Integrated form $$ [A](t) = [A]_0\,e^{-kt} \qquad \ln[A] = \ln[A]_0 - kt $$

A plot of \(\ln[A]\) versus \(t\) is therefore linear with slope \(-k\) — this is the diagnostic test for first-order behavior. The half-life, \(t_{1/2} = \ln 2 / k\), is independent of starting concentration; this is a hallmark property unique to first-order processes.

Chapter 02

Second-order kinetics.

If the elementary event requires two molecules to meet, the rate is bilinear. For \( A + A \rightarrow P \) (or any process second-order in a single species),

$$ -\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k\,[A]^{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{[A]} = \frac{1}{[A]_0} + kt $$

The diagnostic plot is now \(1/[A]\) versus \(t\), linear with slope \(+k\). The half-life is \( t_{1/2} = 1/(k[A]_0) \) — it grows as the reaction proceeds, because the dwindling concentration makes successful collisions rarer.

For the mixed case \(A + B \rightarrow P\) with \([A]_0 \ne [B]_0\),

$$ \frac{1}{[B]_0 - [A]_0}\,\ln\!\frac{[A]_0\,[B]}{[B]_0\,[A]} = kt $$

Chapter 03

Sequential reactions & the rate-determining step.

Real chemistry seldom takes place in a single step. Consider the simplest sequence,

$$ A \xrightarrow{k_1} B \xrightarrow{k_2} C $$

The coupled differential equations

$$ \frac{d[A]}{dt} = -k_1[A],\quad \frac{d[B]}{dt} = k_1[A] - k_2[B],\quad \frac{d[C]}{dt} = k_2[B] $$

admit closed-form solutions (for \(k_1 \ne k_2\)):

$$ [A] = [A]_0\,e^{-k_1 t} $$ $$ [B] = \frac{k_1[A]_0}{k_2 - k_1}\!\left(e^{-k_1 t} - e^{-k_2 t}\right) $$ $$ [C] = [A]_0\!\left[1 + \frac{k_1\,e^{-k_2 t} - k_2\,e^{-k_1 t}}{k_2 - k_1}\right] $$

The intermediate \(B\) rises, peaks, and falls. The position of the peak depends sensitively on the ratio \(k_2/k_1\). The slower step is the rate-determining step (RDS): it controls the overall production of \(C\). When \(k_2 \gg k_1\), \(B\) is consumed nearly as fast as it forms — its concentration stays small and the rate of formation of \(C\) tracks the slow first step.

Chapter 04

The steady-state approximation.

When an intermediate is highly reactive — that is, when \(k_2 \gg k_1\) in the scheme above — its concentration rises to a small, slowly-varying value. After a short induction period, formation and consumption of the intermediate are nearly balanced:

SSA $$ \frac{d[B]_{\text{ss}}}{dt} \approx 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_1[A] = k_2[B]_{\text{ss}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [B]_{\text{ss}} = \frac{k_1}{k_2}[A] $$

The steady-state approximation (SSA) replaces a stiff differential equation with an algebraic relation, and reduces the overall rate to that of the slow step:

$$ \frac{d[C]}{dt} = k_2[B]_{\text{ss}} = k_1[A] $$

Try increasing \(k_2\) below and watch the exact \([B](t)\) curve flatten into a steady-state plateau that hugs the \( (k_1/k_2)[A] \) prediction (dashed). The approximation breaks down when the two rate constants are comparable.

Classic application — Lindemann mechanism. A unimolecular gas-phase reaction \( A \rightarrow P \) actually proceeds via collisional activation: \( A + M \overset{k_1}{\underset{k_{-1}}{\rightleftharpoons}} A^{*} + M\), then \( A^{*} \xrightarrow{k_2} P \). Applying the SSA to \(A^{*}\) gives \( r = \dfrac{k_1 k_2 [A][M]}{k_{-1}[M] + k_2} \), which becomes first-order at high pressure (\(k_{-1}[M] \gg k_2\)) and second-order at low pressure — exactly as observed.
Chapter 05

Parallel (competing) reactions.

A single reactant may have more than one fate:

$$ B \xleftarrow{k_1} A \xrightarrow{k_2} C $$

The reactant decays at the combined rate, and the two products accumulate in a fixed ratio set by the rate-constant ratio — the kinetic branching ratio:

$$ [A] = [A]_0\,e^{-(k_1+k_2)t},\qquad \frac{[B]}{[C]} = \frac{k_1}{k_2}\ \text{at all times} $$

This independence from time is conceptually important. In synthesis, controlling selectivity often means tuning the activation energies of competing channels so that \(k_1/k_2\) shifts in the desired direction — a temperature change, a catalyst, or a solvent swap may all do it.

Chapter 06

Reversible reactions & the approach to equilibrium.

No reaction is truly one-way. For the elementary equilibrium

$$ A \underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}} B $$

the net rate is the difference of forward and reverse contributions:

$$ \frac{d[A]}{dt} = -k_1[A] + k_{-1}[B] $$

At equilibrium the two are equal, which immediately gives the connection to thermodynamics:

$$ k_1[A]_{\text{eq}} = k_{-1}[B]_{\text{eq}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_{\text{eq}} = \frac{[B]_{\text{eq}}}{[A]_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{k_1}{k_{-1}} $$

Solving the coupled equations (with \([B]_0 = 0\)) yields an exponential approach with a relaxation rate constant equal to the sum:

$$ [A](t) - [A]_{\text{eq}} = ([A]_0 - [A]_{\text{eq}})\,e^{-(k_1 + k_{-1})t} $$

This is why temperature-jump and stopped-flow experiments measure \(k_1 + k_{-1}\): the system "forgets" its perturbation at the relaxation rate \(\tau^{-1} = k_1 + k_{-1}\), not at either constant alone.

Note the striking result that the same equilibrium can be reached more quickly when both \(k_1\) and \(k_{-1}\) are larger (even at fixed \(K_\text{eq}\)). Catalysts exploit this: they accelerate forward and reverse equally, shortening the time to equilibrium without changing where equilibrium lies.
Chapter 07

Temperature dependence — the Arrhenius equation.

Empirically, rate constants depend on temperature in a remarkably consistent way, first quantified by Svante Arrhenius in 1889:

$$ k(T) = A\,e^{-E_a / RT} $$

Here \(E_a\) is the activation energy (the height of the barrier separating reactants from products), \(A\) is the pre-exponential factor (capturing collision frequency and steric factors), and \(R\) is the gas constant. Taking logarithms gives the diagnostic linear form:

$$ \ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{R}\cdot\frac{1}{T} $$

An Arrhenius plot of \(\ln k\) versus \(1/T\) is linear, with slope \(-E_a/R\) and intercept \(\ln A\). The two-point form is useful in practice:

$$ \ln\!\frac{k_2}{k_1} = -\frac{E_a}{R}\!\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right) $$

A rule of thumb: for typical organic reactions \(E_a \approx 50\,\text{kJ/mol}\), a 10 K rise near room temperature roughly doubles \(k\).

Linear regression on \(\ln k\) vs \(1/T\) gives slope \(\approx -13{,}400\,\text{K}\), hence \(E_a = -R \cdot \text{slope} \approx 111\,\text{kJ/mol}\), and pre-factor \(A \approx 1.5\times 10^{12}\,\text{s}^{-1}\) — a typical value for a unimolecular-like transition state.

Worked example. If a reaction has \(E_a = 75\,\text{kJ/mol}\) and \(k = 1.0\times 10^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}\) at 298 K, what is \(k\) at 318 K? Using the two-point form:
\( \ln(k_2/k_1) = -(75000/8.314)(1/318 - 1/298) = -9023 \times (-2.11\times 10^{-4}) = +1.905 \). So \(k_2/k_1 = e^{1.905} \approx 6.72\), giving \(k_2 \approx 6.7\times 10^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}\) — roughly a seven-fold acceleration for a 20 K rise.
Coda

What you've seen.

Each panel above is an instance of the same intellectual move: write down the rate law for an elementary process, integrate it (or apply a simplifying approximation when integration is intractable), and connect the resulting time courses to observable quantities — half-lives, diagnostic linear plots, branching ratios, equilibrium constants, activation energies. The whole edifice of physical and biological kinetics — from radioactive decay to drug metabolism to atmospheric ozone chemistry — is built on these primitives.

From here, the natural next steps are transition-state theory (a microscopic justification of Arrhenius), enzyme kinetics (Michaelis–Menten as a steady-state result), and chain reactions and explosions (where multiple intermediates and feedback loops produce qualitatively new behavior). The differential equations get richer, but the methodology stays the same.
