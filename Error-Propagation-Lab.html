Uncertainty Analysis

Error Propagation Lab

How measurement uncertainties in a, b, c… carry through a calculation into the uncertainty of a result f. Learn the rules, then propagate any formula numerically.

σf = √( Σi (∂f/∂xi)² σxi² )

Why errors propagate

Every measured quantity carries an uncertainty — a range around the reported value that reflects the limits of the instrument, the reader, or the method. When measured quantities are combined in a calculation (added, multiplied, raised to a power, fed into a function…) their uncertainties combine too. The result is never more precise than the least well-known ingredient allows.

The question this lab answers: given a, b, c… each with its own uncertainty σa, σb, σc…, what is the uncertainty of some function f(a, b, c, …)?

The general rule

For independent, randomly-distributed (uncorrelated) uncertainties, the variance of f is the sum of the variances contributed by each variable, each weighted by how sensitively f responds to that variable — its partial derivative:

σf = √( (∂f/∂a)²σa² + (∂f/∂b)²σb² + … )

This single formula is the source of every "shortcut" rule below — each one is just this equation evaluated for a particular f. The Calculator tab applies it directly (numerically) to whatever formula you type in, so you never have to differentiate by hand.

Notice the pattern: for addition/subtraction, absolute uncertainties add in quadrature. For multiplication/division, relative (fractional) uncertainties add in quadrature. Quadrature — "square, sum, square-root" — is what makes the largest uncertainty dominate the result and small ones matter much less than you'd guess from adding them directly.

Worked example — density

A mass m = 12.40 ± 0.02 g occupies a volume V = 5.31 ± 0.03 cm³. Density ρ = m / V is a quotient, so relative uncertainties add in quadrature:

Central value
ρ = 12.40 / 5.31 = 2.3352 g/cm³
Relative uncertainties
σm/m = 0.00161  σV/V = 0.00565
Combine in quadrature
√(0.00161² + 0.00565²) = 0.00587
Reported result
ρ = 2.335 ± 0.014 g/cm³

The volume's 0.57% relative uncertainty dominates over the mass's 0.16% — note it isn't simply the larger of the two that "wins", quadrature blends them, but the smaller one barely moves the answer. Try the same numbers in the Calculator tab (preset "a / b") to check.

Common pitfalls

Correlated variables. The formulas above assume a, b, c… are independent. If two quantities share a common source of error (e.g. both read from the same miscalibrated scale), the true uncertainty can be larger or smaller than quadrature predicts — a covariance term is needed.
Quadrature vs. worst case. Simply adding uncertainties (σa + σb) gives the "worst possible" error, not the statistically expected one, and is almost always too pessimistic for independent random errors. Use quadrature unless you specifically want a worst-case bound.
Systematic errors. A systematic offset that shifts every reading the same way does not average out and should generally be tracked separately from — not combined in quadrature with — random uncertainty.
Over-precise reporting. An uncertainty is rarely known to better than 1–2 significant figures, so quoting σ = 0.0138472 is misleading. Round the uncertainty first (usually to 1 sig. fig., or 2 if the leading digit is 1), then round the value to match — e.g. 2.335 ± 0.014, not 2.33520 ± 0.0138472.
