EPR Guide & reference

1. The big picture

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, also ESR) detects species with unpaired electrons: organic radicals, radical ions, transition-metal ions (Cu²⁺, VO²⁺, Mn²⁺, Fe³⁺, Ti³⁺, Co²⁺ …), lanthanides, triplet and photoexcited states, paramagnetic point defects in solids, conduction electrons. It is the electron analogue of NMR: a static magnetic field splits the spin levels and microwaves drive transitions between them. Diamagnetic matter is invisible, which makes the technique very selective.

A spectrum is read for four things: where it lies (g-value: identity and electronic structure), how it splits (hyperfine coupling A: which nuclei, how much spin density; zero-field splitting D, E for S > ½), how wide it is (relaxation, dynamics, exchange, unresolved couplings) and how big (double integral ∝ number of spins).

2. Resonance condition and units

h·ν = g·μB·B   ⇒   B(mT) = 71.4477 · ν(GHz) / g    g = 71.4477 · ν(GHz) / B(mT)

μB/h = 13.9962 GHz/T. For a free electron ge = 2.00232, so 1 mT corresponds to 28.025 MHz (1 mT = 10 G). EPR is normally recorded at fixed frequency while the field is swept, because the microwave cavity only works at its resonant frequency. Because the two spin levels are almost equally populated (n↑/n↓ = exp(−hν/kT), a 0.08 % excess at X-band and room temperature) EPR is intrinsically less sensitive than one would like; low temperature and higher frequency both help.

Splittings in mT depend on the frequency only through g: hyperfine couplings in mT are (nearly) frequency independent, whereas g-anisotropy spreads out in proportion to the field. That is why moving from X- to Q- or W-band separates g-anisotropy from hyperfine structure; the header frequency control lets you try this on every tab.

3. The g-value

g = ge + Δg, where Δg comes from spin–orbit coupling mixing excited states into the ground state: Δg ∝ λ / ΔE. Light-element organic radicals have Δg ≈ 0 to +0.005 (g ≈ 2.002–2.006); heavier atoms (S, Cl) and low-lying excited states push g further away. For d-block ions, g tells you the d-orbital ground state: Cu²⁺ in an elongated octahedron (dx²−y²) has g∥ > g⊥ > 2.0023; a dz² ground state gives g⊥ > g∥ ≈ 2.0023. Less than half-filled shells (VO²⁺, Ti³⁺) give g < ge; more than half-filled give g > ge.

g is a tensor: g(n) = √(gx²nx² + gy²ny² + gz²nz²) for field direction n (in the principal-axis frame). In solution fast tumbling averages it to giso = (gx+gy+gz)/3; in solids, frozen solution and powders you see the anisotropy.

4. Hyperfine coupling

A nucleus with spin I splits each electron transition into 2I+1 lines of equal intensity. n equivalent nuclei give 2nI+1 lines: for I = ½ the intensities follow Pascal's triangle (1:1, 1:2:1, 1:3:3:1 …), for I = 1 (¹⁴N, ²H) the trinomial 1:1:1, 1:2:3:2:1 …. Inequivalent sets multiply: total lines = ∏(2nᵢIᵢ+1) (before accidental overlap). The splitting (a or A) is quoted in mT, G or MHz.

Aiso (Fermi contact) ∝ |ψ(0)|² — s-orbital spin density at the nucleus
McConnell (π radicals): aH = Q·ρπ, |Q| ≈ 2.3 mT

Hence the benzene radical anion (spin density 1/6 per carbon) has a(H) = 2.3/6 = 0.375 mT (seven lines, 1:6:15:20:15:6:1), and the methyl radical, with ρ = 1 on C, has a(3H) ≈ 2.3 mT (1:3:3:1). Isotropic couplings come from s-character; dipolar couplings (traceless tensor, ∝ 1/r³) average to zero in solution but give the anisotropy of A in solids. Nuclei like ¹²C, ¹⁶O, ³²S have I = 0 and are invisible, but isotopes (¹³C 1.1 %, ³³S, ¹⁷O) give weak satellites.

5. Anisotropy and powder patterns

A randomly oriented powder or frozen solution contains every orientation; the spectrum is the sum of single-crystal spectra, weighted by the number of crystallites at each angle (∝ sin θ) and by the transition probability. The result is a characteristic envelope whose turning points sit at the principal g-values (the field positions B = hν/gμB for gx, gy, gz):

  • Isotropic: one symmetric derivative line.
  • Axial (gx = gy = g⊥, gz = g∥): g∥ > g⊥ gives a weak step at low field (g∥) and a strong feature at high field (g⊥). g⊥ > g∥ gives the mirror image.
  • Rhombic (three different g): three features, the middle one (gy) being the derivative zero-crossing at the peak of the powder absorption.

With hyperfine, each principal feature splits into 2I+1 lines with spacing A∥ or A⊥. For axial Cu²⁺ you see four resolved lines on the g∥ region (A∥ ≈ 15–20 mT) and a poorly resolved group near g⊥ (A⊥ ≈ 1–3 mT). Remember: A in MHz relates to the field splitting by ΔB = A / (g·13.996 MHz/mT) using the g of that orientation, not ge.

6. S > ½: zero-field splitting

For S ≥ 1 the spins interact with each other (dipolar) and with the orbital motion, giving a zero-field splitting: H = D[Sz² − S(S+1)/3] + E(Sx² − Sy²). Even with no field the levels are split. For S = 1 (triplets, Ni²⁺ …) the two allowed transitions split by ±(3/2)(n·D·n)/gμB around the free-spin position; powder patterns are Pake-like with turning points where B ∥ x, y, z. The outer z-lines are 2D/gμB apart, so D is read off directly. A forbidden Δm = 2 transition appears near half field (g ≈ 4), used to identify triplets and biradicals. If D is much larger than hν (≳ 1 cm⁻¹ at X-band) the allowed transitions cannot be reached; use higher frequency (HFEPR) or conclude "EPR silent" (many non-Kramers ions).

Kramers vs non-Kramers: ions with an odd number of electrons (S half-integer: Cu²⁺, Mn²⁺, Fe³⁺, Co²⁺ …) always keep a doubly degenerate level in zero field (Kramers doublets) and are generally EPR active. Even-electron integer-spin ions (Fe²⁺, Mn³⁺, Ni²⁺ …) may be EPR silent at conventional frequencies. For S = 5/2 with a large rhombic ZFS (E/D ≈ 1/3), the middle doublet gives the famous isotropic effective g ≈ 4.3 signal of high-spin Fe³⁺; weak ZFS gives g ≈ 2 with a sextet of ⁵⁵Mn lines for Mn²⁺.

7. Linewidth, relaxation, exchange

Quoted as the peak-to-peak derivative width ΔBpp. Lorentzian lines (ΔBpp = 2Γ/√3) come from homogeneous broadening — spin–lattice (T₁) and spin–spin (T₂) relaxation, exchange, collisions; Gaussian lines (ΔBpp = 2σ) from unresolved hyperfine structure or distributions of g and A (strain). Spin-exchange between radicals in concentrated solution broadens and finally collapses hyperfine structure into one line (exchange narrowing). In nitroxides, tumbling rate changes the three lines' widths differently (high-field line first), a standard probe of rotational correlation time. Oxygen (paramagnetic) broadens lines in solution — degas for sharp spectra.

8. The experiment

  • Field modulation and first derivative. The field is modulated (usually 100 kHz) and the lock-in detector picks out the component in phase with it, i.e. the slope of the absorption line. This removes baseline noise but means the signal is a derivative. Modulation amplitude must stay below about one third of the narrowest linewidth; otherwise lines are broadened and distorted (try it in the Experiment tab).
  • Microwave power. Signal ∝ √P at low power, then saturates when B₁ is strong enough that spins cannot relax back (s = γ²B₁²T₁T₂). Beyond saturation the signal falls and lines broaden by √(1+s). Slow-relaxing radicals saturate at μW; fast-relaxing metal ions can need 100 mW at low temperature. Power-saturation curves give relaxation information.
  • Quantification. Intensity is proportional to the area under the absorption curve, i.e. the double integral of the recorded derivative. Compare with a standard (e.g. CuSO₄, TEMPO, DPPH, Mn²⁺ in MgO), measured under identical conditions.
  • g-calibration. Check the field/frequency calibration with a g standard; DPPH (g = 2.0036) is common; use ν counter, not nominal frequency.

9. Reading an unknown spectrum — workflow

  1. Frequency, field range, and g. Where is the centre of gravity? Is g near 2.00 (organic radical or isotropic S=½), far from it (metal), or ≈ 4.3 / 6 (high-spin)?
  2. Width of the whole signal. A few mT: radical. 50–200 mT: metal complex or many unresolved lines.
  3. Shape: isotropic, axial or rhombic? Count turning points and note which is stronger. This gives the symmetry and the orbital ground state.
  4. Hyperfine lines. Count lines, measure spacing, check intensity ratios. Lines = 2nI+1; ratios give n and I. Four lines → I = 3/2 (Cu); six → ⁵⁵Mn; eight → ⁵¹V; three → ¹⁴N.
  5. Extra features. Half-field line (triplet), satellites (isotopes), ligand superhyperfine structure (¹⁴N on Cu²⁺: nine/seven lines).
  6. Dependence on conditions. Temperature (Curie law: intensity ∝ 1/T; broadening at high T), power saturation, solvent/pH.
  7. Simulate and compare. Fit g, A, D, linewidth with a simulation program (EasySpin, SIMPOW, WinEPR). Frequency-sweeps at two bands break ambiguities.
  8. Quantify by double integration.

11. What this app simulates (and doesn't)

  • S = ½ with one nucleus and a tensor g and A with collinear axes, to first order in the hyperfine coupling. Second-order shifts (visible for large A at low frequency: unequal spacings, extra intensity differences between lines) are ignored. Transition-probability anisotropy is included for g.
  • S = 1 with exact diagonalisation of the 3×3 spin Hamiltonian (g isotropic, any D, E), no hyperfine.
  • Isotropic hyperfine from several sets of equivalent nuclei in the fast-motion limit (exact stick positions; no relaxation-induced linewidth alternation).
  • Lineshapes are Gaussian/Lorentzian mixtures with constant linewidth; no strain broadening (mI-dependent widths), no g/A distribution.
  • No pulse EPR, ENDOR, exchange or dynamics, no S > 1 spin Hamiltonians (high-spin ions are shown by effective g-values only).
