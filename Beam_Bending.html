Guide — bending of beams

A short course in the physics behind this app, plus things to try. Everything uses millimetres, newtons and megapascals (E is entered in GPa).

1 · What bends, and why

When a beam is loaded sideways its upper and lower faces stretch and shorten by different amounts. One layer in between, the neutral axis, keeps its length. The strain at distance y from it is ε = −y/R, where R is the radius of curvature. Hooke's law gives the stress σ = Eε, and summing the moment of that stress over the cross-section gives the central result of beam theory:

M = E · I · κ    (κ = 1/R = d²δ/dz²)    σ = M·y / I

EI is the flexural rigidity. It is the only way the material (E) and the shape (I) enter the problem, and they enter as a product. Doubling E and doubling I are equivalent for deflection. For stress they are not: σ = My/I does not contain E, so a stiffer material is not automatically a stronger one.

2 · Young's modulus E

E = stress / strain in the linear elastic region (GPa). It measures resistance to elastic stretch: rubber 0.01, polymers 2–4, wood along the grain ≈ 11, aluminium 69, steel 200, diamond ≈ 1050 GPa. A bending test is one of the easiest ways to measure it: load a bar of known size, measure the deflection, and rearrange the formulas below for E. The Measure E & errors tab does this and shows which dimension limits the accuracy.

3 · Cantilever (fixed at one end)

The beam is clamped at z = 0 and loaded with a point force P at distance a from the clamp. The bending moment is largest at the wall, Mmax = P·a, and falls linearly to zero at the load.

δ(z) = P z² (3a − z) / (6EI)   for z ≤ a
δ(z) = P a² (3z − a) / (6EI)   for z ≥ a (straight beyond the load)
Load at the tip (a = L):   δtip = P L³ / (3EI)

Notice the L³: doubling the length makes the tip 8× floppier. Moving the load inward lowers both the moment and the deflection very quickly (a² (3L − a)).

4 · Three-point bending (simply supported)

The beam rests on two supports a distance L apart and is pushed down by a nose at distance a from the left support (b = L − a from the right). The reactions are R₁ = Pb/L and R₂ = Pa/L. The moment peaks under the load, Mmax = P a b / L, and the lower face is in tension.

Load at the centre:   δmax = P L³ / (48 EI),   Mmax = P L / 4
Any position:   δ(at the load) = P a² b² / (3 E I L)

For an off-centre load the largest deflection is not under the load but in the longer segment, a little way towards the middle. The Diagrams tab shows where. With "own weight" ticked, a uniform load w = ρ·A·g is added by superposition.

5 · Area moment of inertia I

I = ∫ y² dA about the neutral axis (through the centroid). It counts area weighted by the square of its distance, so material far from the axis is worth far more than material near it. That is why I-beams and tubes work, and why a ruler is floppy flat but stiff on edge.

The app computes I (and Iyy, Ixy) numerically for every shape from its outline using Green's theorem, so composite and rotated shapes need no special formulas; the closed forms above are shown where they exist and agree with the numbers. Two rules sit behind the numbers: the parallel-axis theorem, I = Ic + A d², and the rotation formulas below. The next few pictures show where all these numbers come from.

5.1 · Slicing into strips: the definition

I = ∫ y² dA says: cut the section into thin strips parallel to the neutral axis, multiply each strip's area dA by the square of its distance y from the axis, and add. For a rectangle every strip has the same width b, so dA = b·dy:

Strips far from the axis (like the yellow one) count for much more than strips near it, because of the y².
I = ∫−h/2+h/2 y² · b dy = b · [y³/3]−h/2+h/2 = b h³ / 12

The h³ is why depth matters so much: doubling the height multiplies I by 8, doubling the width only by 2.

5.2 · Strips of changing width: the triangle

For a triangle the strip width shrinks linearly with height. Measuring y up from the base, w(y) = b (1 − y/h), so dA = b (1 − y/h) dy. About the base:

The strip gets narrower as y grows; the centroid (blue) lies one third of the height above the base.
Ibase = ∫0h y² · b(1 − y/h) dy = b (h³/3 − h³/4) = b h³ / 12
Move to the centroid axis, a distance h/3 away, with the parallel-axis theorem (below):
Ic = b h³/12 − (bh/2)(h/3)² = b h³ / 36

5.3 · Moving the axis: the parallel-axis theorem

If you know I about the centroid and want it about another, parallel axis a distance d away, every strip is further from the new axis, and the sum picks up an extra A·d²:

The correction A d² is always positive: I is smallest about the centroid.

5.4 · Circles: rings and the polar moment

Strips are awkward for a circle, but concentric rings are easy. A ring at radius r with thickness dr has area dA = 2πr dr, and every part of it is the same distance r from the centre, so the polar moment is J = ∫ r² dA. Because x² + y² = r², J = Ix + Iy, and by symmetry Ix = Iy = J/2.

5.5 · Irregular shapes: the rectangle-and-triangle method

Most real cross-sections are not in any table, but nearly all can be cut into rectangles, triangles (and circles) whose I is known. The method is mechanical:

  1. Split the section into simple pieces. Holes become pieces with negative area.
  2. For each piece write down its area Ai, the height ȳi of its own centroid above a convenient reference line (e.g. the bottom), and its own Ic,i from the table below.
  3. Find the centroid of the whole section: ȳ = Σ Ai ȳi / Σ Ai.
  4. Move every piece to that axis with the parallel-axis theorem and add: I = Σ ( Ic,i + Ai di² ), where di = ȳi − ȳ.

The classical trapezoid formula h³(a² + 4ab + b²) / [36(a + b)] gives the same 20.8×10⁶ mm⁴. The same four steps work for any outline the app does not offer; the app itself uses the more general triangle method described next.

5.6 · Any straight-edged outline: the triangle fan (what the app does)

The app does not need a library of shapes. It takes the outline as a polygon (a circle becomes 360 short edges) and joins every edge to the origin, which gives one triangle per edge. Each triangle adds a signed area and a signed moment, and the signs make the overlaps cancel, so the origin can be anywhere, even outside the shape. Holes are simply loops traced the other way round.

One triangle per edge, all sharing the origin O. For the edge from (x₀, y₀) to (x₁, y₁):
c = x₀y₁ − x₁y₀    ΔA = c / 2    ΔIx = (y₀² + y₀y₁ + y₁²) c / 12    ΔIy = (x₀² + x₀x₁ + x₁²) c / 12    ΔIxy = (x₀y₁ + 2x₀y₀ + 2x₁y₁ + x₁y₀) c / 24

Summing over all edges gives A, the first moments (hence the centroid) and the moments about the origin; the parallel-axis theorem then moves them to the centroid, exactly as in step 4 above. This is the same idea as the rectangle-and-triangle method, only with many more, thinner triangles, and it also delivers the product of inertia Ixy = ∫ xy dA that unsymmetric bending needs.

6 · Rotating the section

Turning the section by θ about its centroid mixes the two second moments:

Ixx(θ) = ½(I₁+I₂) + ½(I₁−I₂) cos 2θ    Ixy(θ) = ½(I₁−I₂) sin 2θ

where I₁ and I₂ are the largest and smallest values, found about the principal axes. A rectangle 20 × 5 mm has I = 20·5³/12 = 208 mm⁴ lying flat but 5·20³/12 = 3333 mm⁴ on edge: 16× stiffer. A circle, a square and a hexagon have I₁ = I₂ so they do not care about orientation; an I-beam or a rectangle do very much. The Orientation sweep tab plots this for the current shape.

7 · Unsymmetric bending

If the load does not lie along a principal axis, the product of inertia Ixy = ∫ xy dA is non-zero and something surprising happens: the beam bends sideways as well as down. For a vertical load the vertical stiffness is not Ixx but

Ieff = (Ixx Iyy − Ixy²) / Iyy    lateral/vertical = Ixy / Iyy    σ = M (Iyyy − Ixyx) / (IxxIyy − Ixy²)

Ieff ≤ Ixx always. The neutral axis (white line in the section view) is not horizontal any more, and the green arrow shows the true direction in which the beam moves. Only when the load is along a principal axis does the beam deflect in the load direction. The app assumes the load passes through the shear centre (no twisting); a real angle or channel loaded off its shear centre would also twist.

8 · Distances from the fulcrum

Everything depends on lengths measured from the support: the moment grows with the lever arm (M = P·a), the deflection with its cube (L³) and, for a cantilever with the load inboard, with a²(3L − a). Drag the load arrow along the beam, or use the probe slider to read shear, moment, slope, deflection and stress at any position z.

9 · Assumptions and limits

  • Euler–Bernoulli theory: plane sections stay plane; shear deformation is ignored (the app warns when L/h is short: < 16 for three-point, < 10 for cantilevers).
  • Small deflection and linear elasticity. Warnings appear above δ/L = 5 % and when σ exceeds the yield/strength value.
  • Isotropic, homogeneous material; wood and CFRP values are along-the-grain/fibre only.
  • Point loads act on the centroid line; local contact stresses under the nose or the supports are not modelled; thin-walled sections are assumed not to buckle or ovalise.
  • The beam is drawn at true length scale; the deflection is exaggerated by the factor shown. The section height is compressed if it would be too fat or too thin to see.

10 · Controls

  • Drag the load arrow on the side view to change a; drag on the section to rotate it; click the Orientation plot to jump to an angle.
  • Choosing a material sets E, ρ and σy; editing E yourself switches to "Custom".
  • The Shape comparison tab rescales every shape to the same area (same mass per length) at the current rotation, so the bars show pure shape efficiency.

11 · Displacement-controlled test (simulation)

Switch the header to Displacement-controlled to mimic a test frame instead of a dead weight. This is the default for both beam types. Cantilever: the clamp (with the beam) is driven down by a chosen distance Δ onto a stationary fulcrum sitting on a load cell, so the beam bends upwards over the fulcrum. Three-point: the two end supports are fixed underneath, and the nose on the load cell is pushed down by Δ, so the bar bends downwards. In both cases the load cell reports the force:

cantilever:   F = k Δ,   k = 3EI / a³     three-point:   k = 3EI L / (a² b²)   (48EI/L³ at midspan)

It is the same bending problem as before with the roles swapped: Δ is the input and F the output. The plot on the right shows F (vertical) against Δ (horizontal) while the test runs. The dashed line is the model k; the slope of your recorded points is fitted live and converted to E. The red marker shows where the peak stress reaches the yield/strength value; “Stop at yield” halts the run there (the model is elastic and does not simulate plastic flow).

  • Run / Pause ramps Δ from 0 to the maximum in the chosen duration; you can also drag the Δ slider by hand (backwards motion is recorded too).
  • Keep trace stores the finished curve in colour so you can change the section, orientation, a or the material and compare the slopes directly. Any change of set-up clears the current trace but keeps the stored ones.
  • Load-cell noise adds Gaussian scatter to the recorded force so you can judge how many points and what range you need for a reliable slope.
  • Overlay data (CSV) reads two columns (displacement in mm, force in N) from your own measurements; Export CSV saves the simulated trace. In the Measure E tab, “Fill” inserts the fitted slope and the table shows which dimension limits the accuracy of E.
  • The beam's own weight is tared out (zeroed), as a load cell would be before the run. The default maximum is the yield displacement or 5 % of a, whichever is smaller, so that small-deflection theory still holds.
