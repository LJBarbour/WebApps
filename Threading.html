Thread Families

An introduction to the four thread standards used in workshop practice, their geometry, and how to navigate this reference.

The Basics

A thread is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. When engaged with a mating thread it converts rotary motion into linear clamping force — or, in the case of pipe threads, forms a pressure-tight seal.

Thread geometry is defined by three key parameters: pitch (crest-to-crest distance), flank angle (60° for metric/SAE/NPT, 55° for BSP), and major diameter (nominal OD). The minor diameter and thread depth follow directly from these.

Coarse vs Fine

Coarse pitch — faster assembly, tolerates dirt and minor damage, preferred in brittle materials and for most structural fastening.

Fine pitch — higher clamp load per unit torque, better vibration resistance, used in thin-walled components, precision adjusters, and automotive work. More sensitive to cross-threading.

Pipe threads — sized by nominal bore, not OD. The actual thread diameter is always larger than the nominal size on the label.

Metric Threads

ISO metric threads — 60° flank angle, pitch in mm. Coarse is the default; fine pitch is used where vibration resistance, higher clamp load, or thin walls are a factor.

Tap drill formula: Tap drill ≈ Major ⌀ − Pitch (gives ~75% thread engagement, standard for most applications). For soft materials or blind holes, 65% engagement (slightly larger drill) reduces tap breakage.
Tolerance classes: 6H/6g is the standard fit for most work. 4H/4h is precision (tighter). 8H/8g is free fit for quick assembly or plated fasteners. Higher class number = looser fit.

Pipe Threads — NPT & BSP

Pipe threads are sized by nominal bore — the actual OD is always larger. Both NPT and BSP follow this convention. Choose standard and type below.

NPT — national pipe taper (ANSI/ASME B1.20.1)

NPT sealing: Taper is 1°47′24″ (1:16 on diameter). Seal forms by thread interference — always use PTFE tape or anaerobic pipe sealant. Typical engagement is 3–6 threads. NPS (straight/parallel) is the non-sealing parallel variant used for free-running mechanical joints.
BSP — british standard pipe (ISO 228 / BS 2779)
Nominal (in)TPIOD (in)OD (mm) Tap drill (mm)Type
BSP sealing: BSP uses a 55° Whitworth thread form. BSPP (G designation, parallel) — seals on a bonded washer or O-ring at the thread face. Never use PTFE tape alone on BSPP. BSPT (Rp/Rc designation, tapered) — seals on threads, similar to NPT. PTFE tape or sealant is appropriate.
NPT vs BSP interchangeability: NPT and BSP are not interchangeable even at the same nominal size. The thread angle (60° vs 55°), OD, and pitch differ. Adapters exist but should not be used in pressure-critical applications.

Match an Existing Threaded Bore
You have a threaded hole and want to turn a matching male part — but you don't know the nominal diameter. Measure across the bore and enter the pitch below — the calculator recovers the nominal size and tells you what to turn to.
What to measure: Bore diameter — place caliper jaws inside the bore. The jaws contact the crests of the female thread — the peaks that point inward, which are the smallest diameter of the bore. This is the female minor diameter. (The female major diameter is the outer groove and cannot be reached by a caliper from inside.) The calculator recovers the nominal from this reading using: nominal = reading + 1.0825 × pitch.

Pitch — use a thread pitch gauge, or count crests over a known length (e.g. 10 crests over ~9 mm = 1.0 mm pitch). For SAE, count threads per inch with a steel rule.

Thread Theory

Geometry, terminology, and special thread forms — the concepts behind the numbers.

Pitch & Lead
Pitch = crest-to-crest distance along the axis. In metric, given in mm; in inch systems, expressed as TPI (1/pitch). Lead = axial advance per full revolution. For a single-start thread, lead = pitch. For multi-start threads (e.g. ACME leadscrews), lead = pitch × number of starts.
Thread Angle
The included angle between the flanks. 60° for Metric, SAE, and NPT — the most common. 55° for BSP and original Whitworth — slightly more rounded form, better for pipe sealing. 29° for ACME power screws. 7°/45° for buttress threads. Lower angles increase axial load capacity but complicate manufacture.
Major, Minor & Pitch ⌀
Major diameter = the nominal OD (crest of external thread). Minor diameter = root of external thread (the smallest diameter). Pitch diameter = the theoretical diameter where thread thickness equals the gap — this is the functional diameter checked by thread gauges. Tolerance is specified at the pitch diameter.
Tolerance & Fit Class
Metric uses tolerance position + grade: 6H/6g is standard; 4H/4h is precision; 8H/8g is free fit for plated or dirty conditions. Uppercase = internal (H), lowercase = external (g, h). SAE uses 1A–3A (external) and 1B–3B (internal). Class 3 is tightest.
Thread Engagement
The axial length over which threads interlock. Rule of thumb: 1× diameter in steel-on-steel; 1.5× in aluminium; 2× in brass, plastic, or cast iron. Beyond 2× engagement, stripping strength is essentially unchanged — adding more thread length gives diminishing returns.
Pipe Thread Sizing
Pipe threads are sized by nominal bore, not OD. A ½″ BSP or NPT fitting has an actual thread OD of ~20.95 mm (BSP) or 21.3 mm (NPT). The nominal size refers to the approximate bore of the pipe it fits — a convention dating to the era of iron pipes with thick walls relative to bore.

Special thread forms
ACME (29°)
Trapezoidal form optimised for power transmission — leadscrews, vises, screw jacks. Easier to machine than square thread. Accommodates split half-nuts for disengagement. General purpose ACME and centralising ACME variants exist.
Square Thread
Maximum mechanical efficiency for power screws — no radial force component means no tendency to spread the nut. Difficult to cut precisely and cannot accommodate half-nuts. Largely replaced by ACME in practice, but still specified in some high-efficiency mechanisms.
Buttress (7° / 45°)
Asymmetric form — one flank near-vertical (7°), the other at 45°. Extremely high axial load in one direction with lower radial force than a square thread. Used in hydraulic cylinders, breech mechanisms, quick-release couplings, and aircraft propeller flanges.
Whitworth (55°)
The original standardised thread, proposed by Joseph Whitworth in 1841. Rounded crest and root, 55° angle. Forms the basis of BSP pipe threads. BSW (coarse) and BSF (fine) designators. Still found in older British machinery and legacy agricultural equipment.
