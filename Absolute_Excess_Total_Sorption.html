Absolute what is physically in the adsorbed layer

Every molecule sitting in the volume next to the surface where the solid's field has raised the density. Needs a model for that volume (or density), so it cannot be measured directly.

Use it for thermodynamics (heats, entropies), isotherm model fitting and comparison with simulation of the adsorbed phase.

Excess what the balance or manometer reports

The adsorbed molecules minus the gas that would have occupied the same volume with no attraction. Needs no model, only a reference volume (usually from helium).

It rises, peaks and falls at high pressure because the bulk gas density keeps growing while the layer saturates.

Total everything inside the pores

Adsorbed layer plus compressed gas filling the remaining pore volume: excess + ρg·Vp. It never falls with pressure.

This is the amount a filled storage tank actually holds. It needs the pore volume Vp.

Common pitfalls to watch for

Same word, different meaning

In molecular simulation (GCMC) “absolute” usually means every molecule in the pore, which is the total here. Simulators then compute excess = N − ρg·VHe. State which convention you use.

Excess depends on the reference volume

The helium (or skeleton) volume defines the zero. A different volume shifts the whole excess isotherm by ρg·ΔV, which grows with pressure.

A falling excess is not desorption

The absolute amount can still be rising or flat while the excess declines. Do not fit Langmuir or Toth to excess data above the maximum without correcting it first.

Absolute needs an assumption

Converting excess to absolute requires the adsorbed-phase density or volume. Liquid density at the boiling point is common but crude, so quote it. Use the Convert tab to see the effect.

Total needs a pore volume

Vp from N₂ at 77 K or CO₂ at 273 K can differ from the volume the working gas reaches. Gas between crystallites in a powder or pellet adds still more.

Near saturation

Below the critical temperature the model is only meaningful under the saturation pressure, since the bulk gas would condense. A warning appears if you go beyond it.

1 · Why are there three numbers?

Put a porous solid in a gas and it takes some up. The question “how much?” is really “how much compared with what?”. Inside a pore there are two kinds of molecule:

  • Near the wall, the solid's attraction packs molecules into a dense adsorbed layer of volume Va.
  • In the middle, the gas is just ordinary gas at the bulk density ρg.

The pore also holds gas that is not adsorbed at all, and the gas density ρg rises steeply with pressure. So you can count:

  • Absolute every molecule in the adsorbed layer,
  • Excess only the molecules beyond what plain gas would put in that same volume,
  • Total every molecule anywhere in the pore.

In reality the layer has no sharp edge. Defining Va is a modelling choice, and that is exactly why the three numbers differ in how well they can be measured.

Excess counts the orange molecules only.
Absolute counts orange + yellow molecules (the whole layer).
Total counts orange + yellow + grey molecules (everything in the pore).

3 · Excess is a subtraction

Imagine the surface is switched off and the layer volume Va is simply filled with bulk gas. It would contain ρg·Va molecules. The real layer contains nabs. Excess is the difference, the molecules the surface really added.

Live: absolute (blue) minus the gas that would fill Va anyway (dashed) gives excess (orange). The dashed line grows with pressure because ρg does, so the subtraction eats into the absolute uptake.

4 · Why does excess go through a maximum?

Differentiate nex = nabs − ρg·Va and the maximum occurs when

dnabs/dP ≈ Va · dρg/dP
  • Low pressure: nabs rises quickly and ρg is tiny, so excess ≈ absolute.
  • Medium pressure: the surface starts to saturate, so nabs flattens, but ρg keeps climbing roughly linearly (more steeply still near the critical point).
  • High pressure: the subtracted term wins and the excess falls. It can even go negative if the bulk gas becomes denser than the adsorbed layer (try the “negative excess” example).

A falling excess does not mean molecules are leaving the pores. The total keeps rising, as the next figure shows. Stronger binding and lower temperature move the maximum to lower pressure, so try the temperature and half-filling sliders.

Live: total (green) is the excess (orange) plus the gas that fills the whole pore (dashed grey), so it is monotonic.

5 · Total is an addition

Everything in the pore volume counts: the adsorbed layer plus the compressed gas in the rest of the pore.

ntot = nabs + ρg·(Vp − Va) = nex + ρg·Vp

This is what a tank filled with the material actually holds, which is why storage targets (for H₂ and CH₄) are stated as total or deliverable amounts. Two cautions: at high pressure much of the total is just ordinary compressed gas, and in a real pellet or powder bed the gas between crystallites adds still more. The excess tells you how much the material contributes over an empty container of the same pore volume.

6 · What does the instrument actually measure?

Both standard methods give the excess, and both need a reference volume from a helium measurement.

In both cases the helium volume plays the role of the reference volume. In the language of Gibbs surface excess, the instrument counts molecules against a bulk-gas background filling VHe. Helium is assumed to enter every pore the working gas can reach and not to adsorb itself. When these assumptions are imperfect the zero of the excess shifts, as the next section shows.

7 · What if the reference volume is wrong?

A mistake ΔV in the helium volume shifts the apparent excess by ρg·ΔV. Since ρg grows with pressure, the error is small at low pressure and large exactly where the excess maximum is.

A positive error can hide the maximum, while a negative error can make the excess fall too early, or even go negative. The same data look like a different isotherm, which is why VHe and the way it was found must be reported, and why helium runs are best done at the measurement temperature or with a correction for any helium uptake.
